Ideas

Where TELEX's hardware could go after the desktop box. The first one is planned in detail; the rest are directions.

CV and gate through an OXI Pipe

TELEX plays MIDI today. A modular rack wants voltages: pitch CV, gates, a clock in. The OXI Pipe is a 6 HP module made for the OXI One sequencer. It has jacks on the front, an HDMI socket on the back, and only wires in between. No video goes through the HDMI cable; OXI uses it as a cheap 19-conductor cable with good plugs. Retailers list 8 CV out, 8 gate out, a clock in and a reset/CV in.

Because the Pipe is passive, the box has to produce finished voltages, as the OXI One does. That means a CV/gate board inside the box, an HDMI socket on its back, and a 12 V supply instead of 5 V so the op-amps can swing the modular range. It's the largest change to the box, which is why it sits here and not in the plan.

Mapping the Pipe

OXI doesn't publish which HDMI pin goes to which jack, so it gets mapped once:

  1. Plug the Pipe's own cable into the Pipe and fit an HDMI breakout board (about $8) to the free end.
  2. Set a multimeter to continuity. Touch each jack's tip and find the pin that beeps. Then the sleeves, for ground.
  3. Check whether ground also runs through the cable's shield.
  4. Record which Pipe version it is. Retailers say the MKII doesn't work with the original OXI One, so the two may be wired differently.

Because the Pipe is only wires, the box decides which way each pin runs. If two inputs turn out to be too few, a few gate jacks could become trigger inputs. The panel labels would be wrong, but the circuit is the box's to choose.

Power for the analog side

The box would run from one 12 V supply. The Teensy's VUSB–VIN pad stays cut, so a computer on the console port and the 12 V supply never feed the same rail.

TELEX words for the modular

Proposed, not built. The names follow Teletype, so Teletype scripts read the same here.

The LFOs that already stream MIDI CC get a CV twin, so an LFO can drive a CV output directly.

What it adds

On top of the box's parts, estimated: a 12 V / 2 A supply, a 12 → 5 V buck and a −12 V inverter (about $25–32), the CV/gate board: an 8-channel 16-bit DAC, op-amps, gate drivers and input protection (about $35–45), and an HDMI socket plus a breakout for mapping (about $10). The Pipe itself is already on hand.

Open questions

  1. Analog rails. Run the op-amps straight from the filtered 12 V input plus a charge-pump −12 V, or use an isolated ±12 V module and regulate down to ±10 V? How much supply noise shows up on 1 V/oct pitch CV?
  2. DAC. An 8-channel 16-bit part (TI DAC8568 or DAC80508), or two cheaper 12-bit MCP4728s? 12 bits over 15 V is about 3.7 mV a step, roughly 4.4 cents at 1 V/oct.
  3. CV range. −5 to +10 V covers pitch and bipolar modulation. Does it match what the OXI One puts out, and what your modules expect?
  4. Gate level. 5 V or 10 V gates, and a transistor per gate or a buffer chip?
  5. Grounding. With the rack on HDMI too, the box shares ground with the computer, the mixer and the rack. How do we keep a loop from humming into the audio?
  6. CV input. Is the Teensy's built-in 12-bit ADC clean enough for the reset/CV input, or does it want a dedicated ADC?
  7. HDMI cable. Is the shield-pin reuse in Figure 2 reliable across ordinary cables, or should the box ship with one known cable?

A box without a screen

The first phase of the build, a Teensy, an audio shield and MIDI on a board, is already an instrument. With no screen and no keys it would be edited from a computer over the USB console (telex.py, or Claude writing scenes) and then left to play. That makes a small, cheap box that sits in a rack or a case and runs one scene all night, with a few knobs for M and the LFO rates if it wants any.

A Eurorack module

The CV/gate board above, a smaller screen and the Teensy could fit behind a panel the width of a Teletype. Typing would move to a USB keyboard on the front, the way Teletype works. It's the furthest idea from what exists, and the one that would most need someone who has built modules before.

More of the box in the browser

The emulator runs the box's firmware compiled for the web. It could load instruments from a folder of WAVs, take MIDI in as well as send it, and save scenes straight to a card file, so the browser becomes a place to write scenes before they go to the hardware.
